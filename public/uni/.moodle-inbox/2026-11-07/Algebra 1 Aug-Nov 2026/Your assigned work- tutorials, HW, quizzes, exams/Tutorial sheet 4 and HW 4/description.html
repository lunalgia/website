1. Basic language. Recall the definition of an abstract vector space. Many terms seen in R^n carry over to this setting: subspace, linear combination, span, linear (in)dependence, basis, dimension. 

  • Prove the basic observations made in class about span, linear dependence/independence and equivalent ways to think of a basis. Mull over them till they become second nature.

We will talk about multisets (sets with repetitions allowed) of vectors. Let S be a multiset in V.

(a) If v is in S and v is a linear combination of the other vectors in S, then span(S) = span(S - {v}). Converse?

(b) If S is independent and v is not in span(S) then S union {v} is independent. Converse?

Let f be a linear map from V to W. State if (c) and (d) are True or False. If false, is there a condition on f under which the statement becomes true?

(c) "If S is linearly independent, then f(S) is linearly independent". Converse?

(d) "If S spans V then f(S) spans W" Converse?

(e) Show that the following are equivalent conditions for a subset B of V. 

1. B is a linearly independent spanning set, i.e., a basis for V by definition.
2. B is a maximal (in terms of set containment) linearly independent set.
3. B is a minimal (in terms of set containment) set that spans V.
4. Each vector in V is a unique linear combination of elements of B.

2. Towards invariance of dimension

Show that if S is a finite spanning set S in a vector space V, then for any linearly independent set I, the cardinality of I is at most the cardinality of S. Give four proofs, first two by contradiction.

(a) Assume |I| > |S|. Express the vectors in I as linear combinations of vectors in S. Express these equations using a wide matrix A by defining a new notion of multiplying a matrix of numbers by a row of abstract vectors. Show appropriate associativity for this new operation and use the fact that Ax = 0 has a nontrivial solution for a wide matrix A.

(b) Assume |I| > |S| and again express the vectors in I as linear combinations of vectors in S. This is a tall system of equations. Imitate row operations on this tall system to get the last equation as 0 = some linear combination of vectors in I. Why must the RHS of this equation be a nontrivial combination?

(c) Proof by reduction to R^n. One way is to use the notion of isomorphism, the fact that having a finite basis for V gives an isomorphism of V with R^n.

(d) Look up the statement of the very clever Steinitz exchange lemma. Try to prove it on your own. Uncover the proof one line at a time and repeat.

3. Payoff. Deduce several consequences of the previous problem for a finite dimensional vector space.

(a) Invariance of dimension (A small but necessary point: we proved that any finite basis of R^n has exactly n elements. What if there is an infinite basis? We need to consider this case too, to assert that the dimension of R^n is n. Can you answer this objection?)

(b) Growing strategy to get a basis: Any linearly independent set can be enlarged to get a basis. (Recall that the cutting down strategy by starting with a given finite spanning set is more basic. This is how we showed the existence of a finite basis for any finite dimensional vector space.)

Note a variation stated in Artin: "If I is linearly independent in V and S spans V, then one can add vectors from S to I to build a basis of V." This is easy to prove on its own. Compare the two proofs. Does one give a stronger statement than the other?

(c) The dimension of a proper subspace W of V  is always less than the dimension of V.

4. Algebra of linear maps and its translation into matrix operations

Imagine that you do not know any operations involving matrices, not even matrix vector multiplication. But you have defined abstract vector spaces and proved the existence of a basis. This gives a dictionary between V of dimension n and R^n, once one has fixed a basis for V, by using the coordinate map. 

Also recall our commutative square where a linear map f: V to W is "translated into a matrix" by using a basis for V and one for W. 

(a) Show that function application f(v) translates into matrix-vector multiplication, so you can define matrix-vector multiplication this way. (This part and the next are souped up versions of 8(a) and (b) on tutorial set 3.)

(b) Show how composition of two linear maps translates into multiplication, of matrices, so you can define matrix multiplication this way. Deduce associativity of matrix multiplication.

(c) We can also scale matrices and add matrices of same size. Moreover matrix multiplication distributes of addition of matrices. What does all this translate to via our dictionary about linear maps? See that what it says is more conceptually and fundamentally understood about linear maps, and then translated to matrices, just like associativity of matrix multiplication.

More precisely, given vector spaces V and W, let L(V,W) be the set of all linear maps from V to W. Show how to make L(V,W) into a vector space and show that this vector space is isomorphic to the vector space of matrices of a certain size. (Hint: choose bases.) Under your isomorphism, which linear map corresponds to the matrix unit E_ij?

5. Computational hook: Let A be the matrix with row 1 = [1,2,3], row 2 = [0,1,4] and row 3 = [5,6,0]. Show that A is invertible, write A as a product of elementary matrices and find the inverse of A.

Theory: Now let $A$ be any square matrix. Show that the following conditions are equivalent:

(a) $A$ is invertible, i.e., there exists a matrix $A'$ such that $AA' = A'A = I$.
(b) The function $f(x) = Ax$ is a bijection.
(c) $A$ has a left inverse $L$, i.e., there exists a matrix $L$ such that $LA = I$.
(d) $A$ has a right inverse $R$, i.e., there exists a matrix $R$ such that $AR = I$.
(e) $\text{RREF}(A) = I$, the identity matrix.
(f) $A$ is a product of elementary matrices.

6. Faux Generality?  

Let $A$ be an $r \times c$ matrix. Throughout this problem, $I$ denotes the identity matrix of the appropriate size that makes the given equation syntactically correct. The problems below, as stated in terms of matrices, are somewhat disguised. The meaning may become more transparent if you think in terms of the associated linear map.

(a) We could call $A$ ``invertible'' if it has a left inverse $L$ and a right inverse $R$. Show that for such an ``invertible'' matrix, one must have $L = R$, and this common inverse is unique. (This small argument is formal and not specific to linear algebra.)

(b) Now suppose $A$ has a (necessarily unique) two-sided inverse $A'$, i.e., $AA' = I$ and $A'A = I$. Note that $A'$ must be a $c \times r$ matrix. Show that if $A$ is invertible in this sense, then we must have $r = c$, so $A$ must be a square matrix after all for it to be invertible.

Let's pursue this line of thinking to see if we get anywhere.

(c)  Computational hook: Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. Show that A has infinitely many right inverses. Find them all. Now give a matrix B having infinitely many right inverses and find them all.

(d) Theory: Characterize non-square matrices that have a left inverse. Show that such a matrix must have infinitely many left inverses. Provide an explicit numerical example illustrating both statements. Do the same for right inverses (characterization, infinitely many right inverses, and an example).

Maybe not so faux after all! (Added: Look also at Artin's Problem M.8 in chapter 1.)

7. About the change of coordinates formula within one vector space.
 
Suppose we are given an old ordered basis B and a new ordered basis B' for a vector space V. Recall that the change of basis matrix P from B to B' is built column by column. The i-th column is the coordinate vector of the i-th NEW basis vector in terms of the old basis B. We saw that in hypervector notation, we have B' = BP. Using this we deduced the following change of coodinates formula:    [v]_B = P[v]_B'     OR     [v]_B' = (P inverse) [v]_B
 
Computational hook: Do as much as you need. 

(a) Let v1 = [1, 2, 1]^T       v2 =  [ 0,1,2 ]^T    v_3 = [2 0 1]^T . Show that B = {v1, v2, v3} is a basis of R^3. What is the change of basis matrix P when old basis is the standard basis and the new basis is B? Find w such that [w]_B = [4,5,5]. Find [v]_B for v = [4,5,5]. 
 
(b) Consider the polynomial $p(t) = 1 + 2t + t^2$, which represents a simple trajectory or curve segment on $t \in [0, 1]$. Find its coordinate vector $[p]_B$ with respect to the Bernstein basis $B = \{b_0(t), b_1(t), b_2(t)\}$ where:
 

  • $b_0(t) = (1-t)^2 = 1 - 2t + t^2$

  • $b_1(t) = 2t(1-t) = 2t - 2t^2$

  • $b_2(t) = t^2$

Search "Bernstein polynomials" or "Bézier curves" to understand the significance for vector graphics.
 
Theory: Return to the general situation in the first paragraph.
 
(c) Justify why P must be invertible. This was discussed very briefly in class. 
 
(d) Two alternative viewpoints about proving the change of basis formulas emerged in discussion after class, both of which avoid the hypervector notation: (i) First, just read off a formula from the equations expressing B' in terms of B. (ii) Think of our commutative square where a linear map f: V to W is "translated into a matrix" by using a basis for V and one for W. Now operationalize this square for the identity map from V to V.
8. Universal property of a basis. Let's see a fifth way to think of a basis by connecting it to linear maps, (in)arguably the more fundamental notion.
 
(a) Suppose B is subset of V. Show (easily) that if B is a basis of V, then for any vector space W, any function f from the set B to W can be uniquely extended to a linear map L from V to W. (Be sure to show that the forced formula actually defines a linear function.)
 
Despite the ease, this property -- called the "universal property" of a basis -- is worth saying because this is how we will frequently exploit a basis.
 
(b) (Optional) Show that the property in part (a) characterizes those subsets of V that are bases of V. This is largely (but not entirely) an exercise in language, in that first you have to formulate what is to be proved. Do you find the verbiage in the formulation worthwhile? No answer is wrong as long as it your authentic answer. Hold on to it and see what you think after two years.
